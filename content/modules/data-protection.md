## Learning objectives

- Recognise personal data, including data that does not show a name.
- Apply the GDPR ideas of purpose limitation and data minimisation when you use an AI tool.
- Know which kinds of personal data need extra care.
- Decide what you may and may not put into an AI tool at work.
- Know what to do straight away if personal data may have leaked.

## Personal data is more than names

Under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), personal data is "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person" (Article 4, point (1)). A person is identifiable if they can be identified directly or indirectly, for example by an identification number, location data or an online identifier.

So a customer number, a car registration, a detailed description of a case, or a photo can be personal data, even without a name.

## The basics that matter when you use AI

The GDPR says personal data must be, among other things (Article 5(1)):

- collected for specified, explicit and legitimate purposes and not further processed in a way that is incompatible with them (**purpose limitation**);
- "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary" for the purpose (**data minimisation**);
- accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date (**accuracy**);
- kept secure (**integrity and confidentiality**).

For you, this means: before you paste personal data into an AI tool, ask whether the task needs it at all, and whether using it this way fits the purpose it was collected for.

## Data that needs extra care

Some personal data is a special category under Article 9 GDPR, and processing it is prohibited unless an exception applies. It includes data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs or trade union membership, genetic data, biometric data used to identify a person, health data, and data about a person's sex life or sexual orientation. Do not put such data into an AI tool unless your organisation has approved that specific use.

## Practical rules

- Use only AI tools that {organisation} has approved for the kind of information you are working with.
- Remove or replace names and other details the task does not need. Replacing a name with a code still leaves personal data if someone can link the code back to the person.
- Do not paste passwords, access keys, unpublished financial results, trade secrets or customer contracts into tools that are not approved for them.
- Check the tool's settings: some tools let you turn off storage of your conversations or their use for training.
- Do not use an AI tool to make decisions about people on your own. Under Article 22 GDPR, people have the right, with some exceptions, not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing that produces legal effects for them or similarly significantly affects them.

## If something goes wrong

If you think personal data went somewhere it should not, for example into an unapproved tool or to the wrong person, tell {ai-contact} or your data protection contact at once. Organisations must notify the supervisory authority of a personal data breach without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware of it, unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to people's rights and freedoms (Article 33 GDPR). Your quick report gives them the time to do that.

::: role builders
### For people building or buying AI

- An AI supplier that processes personal data for your organisation is usually a processor. Your organisation may use only processors that provide sufficient guarantees, and needs a contract that meets Article 28 GDPR.
- Where processing, in particular using new technologies, is likely to result in a high risk to people's rights and freedoms, a data protection impact assessment is needed before processing starts (Article 35 GDPR). Many AI uses involving personal data fall into this area; check with your data protection officer.
- Design for minimisation: send the AI only the fields the task needs, and log as little as possible.
:::

::: role managers
### For managers

Make the approved tools easy to find and use. When people have no approved option, they turn to unapproved ones. Agree with your data protection contact which kinds of data each tool may receive, and say it in plain words.
:::

## Quiz

### Question data-protection-1
Which of these is personal data?

- [ ] The average sales figure of a whole country.
- [x] A customer number that your systems link to one individual customer.
- [ ] The text of a published law.

Explanation: Information is personal data if it relates to a person who can be identified directly or indirectly, for example by an identification number (Article 4, point (1) GDPR).

### Question data-protection-2
You want an AI tool to improve the wording of a complaint letter about a named customer's medical condition. What is the best approach?

- [ ] Paste the full letter into any free AI tool. It is only about wording.
- [x] Remove the name and health details the task does not need, and use only a tool approved for this kind of data.
- [ ] Add more detail so the AI understands the case better.

Explanation: Health data is a special category (Article 9 GDPR). Data minimisation means using only what the task needs, and only in tools your organisation has approved.

### Question data-protection-3
You replaced customer names with codes before using an AI tool. Your team keeps a list that links each code to a name. Is the data still personal data?

- [x] Yes. People can still be identified with the list.
- [ ] No. Codes are never personal data.
- [ ] Only if the AI tool knows about the list.

Explanation: Replacing names with codes (pseudonymisation) reduces risk, but for your organisation, which holds the list, the data can still be linked back to each person, so it is still personal data (GDPR recital 26).

### Question data-protection-4
You realise you pasted a file with employees' home addresses into an AI tool that is not approved. What do you do first?

- [ ] Nothing. The tool probably deletes it.
- [ ] Wait to see whether anyone complains.
- [x] Tell your AI or data protection contact at once.

Explanation: This may be a personal data breach. Organisations must notify the authority without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk (Article 33 GDPR). They can only do that if they know.

### Question data-protection-5
What does "data minimisation" mean?

- [ ] Compressing files so they use less storage.
- [x] Using only personal data that is adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary for the purpose.
- [ ] Deleting all data after one day.

Explanation: Article 5(1), point (c) GDPR.

### Question data-protection-6
Roles: builders

A cloud AI service will process your customers' personal data on your organisation's behalf. What do you need?

- [ ] Nothing, if the service is popular.
- [x] A supplier that gives sufficient guarantees and a processing contract that meets Article 28 GDPR.
- [ ] Only the supplier's marketing brochure.

Explanation: A service that processes personal data on your behalf is a processor. Article 28 GDPR sets out what you must check and agree.

### Question data-protection-7
Roles: managers

Your team keeps using a free AI tool that is not approved. What is the most useful first step?

- [ ] Ban all AI tools.
- [x] Find out what they need it for, and provide an approved tool with clear rules on which data it may receive.
- [ ] Ignore it, since it saves time.

Explanation: People use unapproved tools when they lack a good approved option. A clear, usable alternative reduces the risk.
